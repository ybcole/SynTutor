## Source and scope

Noel Burton-Roberts, *Analysing Sentences*, third edition, Pearson, 2011, ISBN 9781408233740. Read printed pp. 67–86 (PDF pp. 82–101) in the same copy used for the Chapter 1 receipt. Visually checked the diagrams on printed pp. 71–73 and 76–78. The current lesson, “The basic transitive VP”, covers the subject/object distinction and the `[trans]` tree. Retain the object-attachment exercise with “Nora inspected the bridge” and extend the explanation to the book’s other five patterns.

| Book section | What the lesson teaches |
| --- | --- |
| A first look at verbs, pp. 67–68, [1–8] | Define a lexical verb and the basic VP. Reconnect with Chapter 2’s subject–predicate split and Chapter 3’s phrase labels. Introduce the lexical/auxiliary distinction in one sentence and defer auxiliary structures and tense to Chapter 6. Defer the word-form classification drill. |
| The complements of lexical verbs, pp. 68–70, [9–18] | Define a complement as a constituent licensed by the verb in a particular use. Contrast a VP with a direct object and a VP that can consist of the verb alone. Use an optional time phrase as a preview of Chapter 5. Explain that a verb can have more than one pattern across uses. |
| Transitive and intransitive verbs, pp. 70–72, [19–22] | Mark the verb’s feature and distinguish the subject NP under S from a direct-object NP beside V under VP. Show an intransitive V as the only immediate part of its basic VP. Defer the full pronoun case comparison and governance terminology. |
| Ditransitive verbs, pp. 72–74, [23–32] | Identify the thing given and the recipient. Compare an indirect-object NP with a corresponding “to” PP after the direct object, using the terminology of this analysis. Mention that some verbs permit a “for” variant. Defer the full verb-by-verb alternation inventory. |
| Intensive verbs, pp. 74–76, [33–46] | Define subject predicative and show how an NP, AP or PP can identify, describe or locate the subject. Compare the same NP serving as subject predicative and as direct object. |
| Complex transitive verbs, pp. 76–78, [48–52] | Define object predicative. Identify the NP direct object and what is said about that object. Use one AP example and mention the NP and PP possibilities. Defer the ambiguous two-NP example [52] so learners can first distinguish the two-complement patterns in clear cases. |
| Prepositional verbs, pp. 78–79, [53–56] | Show a PP licensed by a particular verb use and contrast it with an intensive PP and an optional location. Use a stable preposition in a clear example. Defer particle constructions and Chapter 5’s modifier attachment. |
| Summary, exercises and discussion, pp. 79–86 | Give a compact six-pattern recap and one transfer comparison. Defer the long passage and further mixed-pattern exercises. |

## Outcomes

1. Find the lexical head and separate the subject from the immediate parts of the basic VP.
2. Compare zero, one and two complements and distinguish the six patterns by category and function.
3. Explain what a predicative describes, and distinguish a PP complement from an optional location detail in clear examples.

## V4 diagram and feedback plan

Use S with subject NP and VP. Put the pattern feature on V. Keep the immediate complements as sisters of V under VP, with NP/AP/PP triangles when internal detail would distract from function. A triangle spans its complete phrase. If the first tree’s object NP is attached to S or V, feedback should direct the learner to the VP parent. Use a different visual state for each numbered trace, including a text outline for the active tree. Keep only one editable Builder on the page. Add a page-specific notation group that explains the features used on that page. The existing page override currently supports categories and symbols only, so extend it during implementation and exercise it in the browser test.

| Example | Immediate parts of VP and expected reading |
| --- | --- |
| Nora inspected the bridge. | `V [trans] + NP`. “The bridge” is the direct object. “Nora” is the subject NP under S. Compare figure [21]. |
| Nora waited. | `V [intrans]`. The verb by itself fills VP in this example. Compare [22]. |
| Mara handed Leo a map. / Mara handed a map to Leo. | `V [ditrans] + NP + NP` or `V [ditrans] + NP + PP`. “A map” is the direct object in both. “Leo” and “to Leo” express the recipient in their respective forms. Compare [26] and [32]. |
| Mina became a coach. / Mina met a coach. | Both have `V + NP`. `[intens]` licenses an NP subject predicative identifying Mina; `[trans]` licenses an NP direct object referring to another person in this example. Compare [46a–b]. |
| The rain left the steps slippery. | `V [complex] + NP + AP`. “The steps” is the direct object, and “slippery” describes it. Compare [51]. |
| Mina relied on her notes. / Mina was in the studio. | `V [prep] + PP` with prepositional complement, or `V [intens] + PP` with subject predicative. Compare [55–56] and [35]. “Mina waited in the studio” offers a separate comparison. “Mina waited” is already a complete clause, and the location adds optional information. |

Use omission as a clue on the selected examples. Also check whether the verb licenses that kind of phrase in that sense, what function the phrase has, and whether it describes a participant or locates the event.

## Draft checks

| Item | Prompt | Answer and feedback focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening 1 | Which word is the main verb in “The wind shook the shutters”? | **shook.** The sentence tells what the wind did. |
| Opening 2 | Which whole group answers “What did Eli pack?” in “Eli packed the red bag”? | **the red bag.** Include the determiner and adjective. |
| Opening 3 | Who receives something in “The courier gave Ada a receipt”? | **Ada.** The receipt is the item given. |
| Opening 4 | What is quiet in “The librarian kept the room quiet”? | **the room.** The description applies to the object. |
| Opening 5 | Which words can be removed from “The visitors rested near the gate” while leaving a complete sentence with the same action? | **near the gate.** The location is optional here. |
| Closing 1 | Compare “The bell rang” and “The caretaker rang the bell”. What is the VP in each sentence, in order? | **“rang” / “rang the bell”.** The same verb is used in two patterns. |
| Closing 2 | In “Ari sent a note to Ren”, what are the functions of “a note” and “to Ren”? | **Direct object and recipient indirect object in PP form.** The thing sent remains the direct object. |
| Closing 3 | In “Nia became a pilot”, what function does “a pilot” have? | **Subject predicative.** The noun phrase identifies Nia after the change. |
| Closing 4 | In “The team painted the door blue”, what does “blue” do in the VP? | **It describes the direct object “the door”.** The VP has an object predicative in addition to its direct object. |
| Closing 5 | In “The recipe consists of three steps”, what role does “of three steps” play? | **Prepositional complement.** “Consists” in this use selects an “of” phrase. |
