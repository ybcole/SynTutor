
# **CHAPTER II**  **REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE** {#chapter-ii-review-of-related-literature}

The Review of Related Literature section examines both foreign and local studies concerning the application of Artificial Intelligence, Natural Language Processing (NLP), and Explainable AI (XAI) in the context of English syntax and grammar analysis. Furthermore, this chapter explores the pedagogical effectiveness of syntax trees in language education alongside existing grammatical learning systems to identify the specific gap that SynTutor aims to address.

## **2.1 FOREIGN LITERATURE** {#2.1-foreign-literature}

Extensive literature in computational linguistics highlights the significant potential of Artificial Intelligence (AI) to enhance linguistic competence. By providing personalized learning experiences, interactive environments, and instantaneous feedback, AI empowers users to refine their language skills with greater efficiency (Rugaiyah, 2023). Given that the future of computer-assisted learning is deeply rooted in these foundations, Natural Language Processing (NLP) and machine learning algorithms are already being extensively applied within the field.

However, for these systems to effectively teach grammar, they must be capable of visualizing and explaining abstract rules to the learner. In linguistics, the syntax tree serves as an effective tool for meeting this need by visualizing the hierarchical structure of a sentence and the relationships between its constituent elements. Understanding these diagrams is crucial for determining phrase structure rules and developing a learner's analytical abilities. Consequently, integrating syntax trees into computational programs—such as a generator that effectively constructs these diagrams—is highly beneficial for both students and linguists (Karjo & Ying, 2024). SynTutor utilizes this approach to provide a critical platform for advancing a learner's linguistic awareness and structural comprehension.

	In a study conducted by Triana et al. (2025), it was determined that AI can substantially assist in the field of syntactic analysis. The researchers sought to validate AI's accuracy using specific sentence patterns, emphasizing its role as a supportive tool rather than a primary authority in syntax education. Their findings revealed that while AI demonstrates a high accuracy rate of 90% in identifying subjects and predicates, it maintains a moderate accuracy of 80% for objects and a lower accuracy of 75% for complements and adverbials. Consequently, AI proves valuable for identifying syntactic patterns, and its integration can offer learners a more comprehensive linguistic understanding. SynTutor adopts a similar framework by utilizing phrase-structure syntax trees to explain sentence structures and improve user comprehension.  
A significant advancement in Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the shift from opaque "black-box" models toward interpretable frameworks, as demonstrated by Li et al. (2023) in their study on explicit syntactic guidance for neural text generation. The researchers proposed a top-down generation schema guided by a constituency parse tree, proving that breaking down sentence construction into hierarchical constituent expansions allows for a more "human-like" and controllable process. Crucially for the development of SynTutor, this syntax-driven approach establishes "interpretable pathways" where errors can be traced back to specific structural depths. SynTutor's architecture directly operationalizes this principle: the benepar_en3 neural constituency parser fixes the phrase-structure tree before any deterministic rule is applied, ensuring that XAI explanations are anchored to a verifiable structure rather than generated opaquely.  
	Ribeiro-Flucht et al. (2024) provide the pedagogical validation for using Explainable AI (XAI) to bridge the "transparency gap" in language instruction. Their research emphasizes that traditional learning models often lack the conceptual clarity necessary for learners to understand why a specific classification or correction was made. The study demonstrates that by employing linguistic complexity measures—specifically phrasal and clausal elaboration—XAI can successfully transform complex data into conceptually understandable explanations. Furthermore, the empirical evidence suggests that XAI methods, such as SHAP, can effectively describe linguistic proficiency for users, thereby encouraging human trust and comprehension. This further validates SynTutor's core objective of using XAI as a transparent educational tool that maps directly to a student's developmental needs.  
	Addressing the critical need for transparency in Large Language Models (LLMs), Li et al. (2025) explored the integration of multiple XAI techniques to enhance model interpretability. Their research indicates that as LLMs become increasingly complex, their black-box nature poses significant risks for accountability, which can be mitigated through a combination of methods such as attention visualization, feature attribution, and probing tasks. The study concludes that no single XAI method provides a comprehensive explanation; rather, a combination of different interpretability tools—visualizing what the model "attends" to while probing its internal linguistic knowledge—creates a more robust understanding of the model's behavior. This research provides the essential framework for SynTutor in developing a transparent feedback system that reveals the "why" behind syntactic rules. By utilizing this diversified XAI strategy, SynTutor transforms the AI from an opaque black box into a transparent digital tutor providing clear, evidence-based guidance. SynTutor's Hybrid Acceptability/Correction-Search channel embodies this multi-faceted approach: the deterministic Linguistic Constraint Matrix supplies rule-level explanations while the CoLA acceptability classifier provides model-level coverage, with the two channels combined rather than allowing either to operate opaquely.   
Muralidaran (2022) developed a linguistically motivated approach to grammar induction that mirrors human psycholinguistic properties such as incrementality and expectation. The research identifies grammatical structures by recognizing general cognitive patterns of conceptual organization, moving away from purely statistical black-box models to prioritize how humans naturally assemble and connect linguistic schemas. This shift toward transparency is central to SynTutor's framework, which rejects black-box corrections in favor of an Explainable AI (XAI) approach. SynTutor aligns with Muralidaran's emphasis on human-like conceptual organization, grounding its feedback in the hierarchical organization of phrase-structure syntax trees so that the AI's reasoning is not just statistically probable but linguistically logical to the learner.  
A survey by Bryant et al. (2023) illustrates how Grammatical Error Correction (GEC) has evolved over time, transitioning from rule-based methods and statistical classifiers to the neural machine translation systems that represent current technological advancements. The paper shows significant progress in identifying and correcting common grammatical mistakes, such as subject-verb agreement and preposition errors, as well as semantic inaccuracies. While current GEC systems achieve high accuracy, they are primarily evaluated on technical precision and recall, with little attention paid to the educational value of the feedback they provide. The authors conclude that while technical performance has greatly improved, these tools often fail as teaching resources because they correct errors without providing explanations. This supports SynTutor's primary assertion that correcting grammar without instructional context prevents learners from developing meaningful grammatical skills. SynTutor specifically addresses this gap by separating detection from explanation: the Deterministic Linguistic Constraint Matrix supplies rule-level "why" while the acceptability classifier provides broad coverage, neither of which operates as an opaque black box.  
According to a study conducted by Bochari and Hastini (2025), it was found that Syntax Tree Diagrams were very beneficial in teaching English as a Foreign Language (EFL) learners to recognize structural ambiguities and resolve them through teaching methods using this tool. The researchers used a pre-experimental one-group pretest/posttest methodology involving thirty-five (35) fifth-semester students from Tadulako University, Indonesia. The results indicated that after learning to parse sentences through tree diagrams, there was a significant improvement in the learners' ability to identify and resolve structural ambiguity, and that using visual representations of syntax in the form of syntax trees is more effective than traditional text-based instruction for improving grammatical understanding. However, the study is limited to classroom-based implementation and does not explore how these visual strategies can be integrated into automated or AI-based systems that provide real-time analysis and feedback, which provides strong support for the use of phrase-structure syntax tree visualization in the SynTutor learning environment.  
Through a socio-technical analysis, Farrow (2023) discusses how explainable AI (XAI) can enhance pedagogical practices by demonstrating that the concept of "explicability" is influenced by many external factors and cannot be viewed as having a singular technical definition. The author identifies three essential components of effective explainability: algorithm transparency, appropriate levels of abstraction in explanations, and sufficient detail. The study also distinguishes between interpretability (human readability) and fidelity (the accuracy of the algorithm's internal operation). Farrow explains that for learners and teachers to effectively interact with AI-based educational systems, explanations must match the user's level of understanding rather than relying on expert-level descriptions. However, since the discussion remains largely theoretical and does not present concrete applications for grammar learning, it reinforces the need for SynTutor to provide clear and learner-friendly explanations within its grammar analysis framework. SynTutor addresses this by mapping Deterministic Linguistic Constraint Matrix violations to plain-language pedagogical templates, ensuring explanations are both technically accurate and learner-accessible.  
Wei (2023) examined the impact of AI-supported language learning on EFL students across multiple areas, including grammar development, vocabulary, reading comprehension, writing ability, and self-regulated learning. In this mixed-methods study involving 60 university participants, the author reported significantly greater improvement for all language learning outcomes among students receiving AI-supported instruction. These results were particularly pronounced in respect to students' performance on grammar-related tasks. Wei explains that these improvements stem from the ability of AI systems to provide immediate, organized, and personalized feedback that increases learner engagement. However, the study does not explore how AI systems can incorporate deeper structural analysis—such as syntax tree visualization and explainable feedback—to further enhance a learner's understanding of sentence construction, which supports the development of SynTutor as a more advanced learning tool.

## **2.2 LOCAL LITERATURE** {#2.2-local-literature}

The development of SynTutor is grounded in local literature that explores both the technical feasibility of automated syntax tree generators and the practical application of AI-driven linguistic tools within the context of the Philippines. Crucially, such a system requires a high-quality parsed corpus for training, Sagum et al. (2023) addressed this infrastructural need by developing an Automated Filipino Language Treebank Generator. Their study focused on automating treebank generation in the Filipino language, where every sentence in a text corpus is parsed and tagged with its syntactic structure. By classifying and tagging words into specific lexical classes, the researchers established an infrastructure for deeper language understanding. This work directly supports SynTutor's technical objectives by demonstrating how word-tag pairs can transition to structural visualizations using automated treebank generation.  
Beyond technical feasibility, local research also highlights the need for creating passive and active structural learning for students, a gap emphasized by Cantilero (2025). The researcher investigated the relationship between syntactic priming—the unconscious learning where students reuse recently encountered structures—speech acts, and the overall linguistic competence of Senior High School students in Aklan. Cantilero's use of priming as a metric found that while students demonstrated high linguistic competency, their level of syntactic priming was low. This finding proves that theoretical knowledge does not directly correlate with the ability to use complex syntactic structures. This gap provides a key rationale for SynTutor, which aims to combine theoretical linguistic knowledge with a visual phrase-structure syntax tree to transform passive knowledge into active structural learning.  
	The question of AI's effectiveness in increasing learner understanding and competence is also explored by local literature. Dela Cruz (2025) investigated the integration of AI into English Classroom Practices in the Philippine higher education system. Analyzing how emerging technologies can address traditional language challenges, the study utilized qualitative and quantitative data from 43 teachers, 158 students, and 3 administrators. The research concluded that AI is effectively used to promote an adaptive and data-informed language education experience. This successful integration is a core tenet of SynTutor, which aims to utilize the same technology to provide interpretable feedback and guide learners toward a deeper understanding of linguistics. Further research also demonstrates that utilizing AI correlates with positive improvements in student performance for English language courses. A descriptive research design identified positive correlations in language improvement, confidence enhancement, and attitude, covering the four core skills of writing, reading, listening, and speaking. Based on these results, researchers have advocated for the use of AI in structurally shaping positive attitudes in learning the English language, which provides evidence for how SynTutor aims to improve a learner's comprehension compared to traditional software and linguistic learning tools.  
A study by Magday et al. (2024) tested the effectiveness of an application integrated with artificial intelligence named "CiCi" to enhance the subject-verb agreement (SVA) skills of learners from the Philippines. Used as a supplemental learning tool, wherein a group consisting of 20 Grade 7 students underwent an intervention program, while another 20 students underwent traditional method of learning of SVA. The results of the study showed that the experimental group that used CiCi significantly improved their skills. Those who were taught through the traditional method did not show any significant improvement in comparison. The study portrays how the use of an AI-integrated tutor contributes in creating innovative teaching strategies, applicable in the field of linguistics and improving a learner's English ability. The themes that the study found: free charges, user-friendly features, fun english learning experiences, and providing fun exercises align with how a similar application like SynTutor can be used for increasing a learner's competence, while utilizing technology.  
	Reinforcing the importance of transforming theoretical knowledge into practical application, Cartilero (2024) similarly evaluates the relationship between syntactic priming, speech acts, and linguistic competence among Senior High School students. This research identified a "low" level of syntactic priming despite "high" linguistic competence, suggesting that students often struggle with the cognitive process of reusing specific sentence structures even if they know the grammatical rules. SynTutor directly addresses this gap. By providing real-time visualization of phrase structures, the system serves as a cognitive scaffold that reinforces syntactic priming, allowing students to move beyond the passive rule-memorization noted by Cartilero and towards an active exploration of how sentence structures are built and reused.  
Furthermore, the importance of building robust computational linguistic resources is highlighted by Arañes (2022), who explored the scarcity of parsed and labeled corpora for the Cebuano language. To address this, the research developed the "GJA Cebuano Treebank" to adapt Universal Dependencies (UD) treebanks for Cebuano, enabling automated text processing and syntactic analysis. SynTutor builds upon this critical development in automated syntax generators by incorporating guidelines for resolving the structural ambiguities that often hinder parsing algorithms. This foundational research thus provides a key framework for a tutoring system designed to explain the hierarchical relationships of sentence structures to learners.  
The need for educational focus over mere correction is reinforced by the systematic literature review of Cano et al. (2024), which evaluated various NLP grammar correction programs. The authors reviewed 40 studies, identifying common algorithms, deep learning methodologies (including Word2Vec, LSTM-CRF, and neural networks), and datasets utilized. While the review proposed improvements to grammar-checking tools, suggesting they incorporate learning support to reduce recurring errors, the authors noted a persistent limitation: existing systems remain largely focused on correction and suggested outputs rather than delivering clear, user-facing explanations that support deeper learning. This finding is highly pertinent to SynTutor, as it outlines the current state of NLP-based grammar checking in Philippine schooling while highlighting the continuing lack of explainable and educational feedback in contemporary applications. SynTutor specifically addresses this limitation through its Deterministic Linguistic Constraint Matrix and Positive-Evidence Lexical Licensing layer, which produce verifiable, rule-based explanations rather than opaque corrections.  
Further supporting the case for advanced AI integration, Soriano et al. (2024) researched the impact of using Meta's AI writing tool(s) on the academic writing skills of 60 Filipino university students in ESL classes. Utilizing a pre-test/post-test design, the data showed statistically significant increases in performance across all measured outcomes (e.g., grammar, coherence, and overall structure), with calculated effect sizes (Cohen's d) representing large improvements for each area. The results, grounded in Cognitive Load Theory and Constructivist Learning Theory, indicate that AI-assisted writing tools boost motivation, engagement, and overall writing performance. However, the study did not examine how these systems provide structured grammatical explanations that promote deeper understanding rather than just performance improvement. This provides local justification for developing SynTutor as a feedback-driven and explanation-based grammar learning tool for Filipino learners.  
Finally, Domingo (2025) investigated the relationship between AI-driven writing tools and Impostor Syndrome among 200 tertiary ESL students at a Philippine state university. Using a mixed-methods explanatory sequential design, the study found that frequent reliance on automated drafting tools resulted in 24.5% of respondents experiencing impostor biases, though only 2.5% indicated intense feelings of impostor syndrome. While AI improved writing self-efficacy and decreased anxiety due to real-time feedback, Domingo warned that excessive reliance could inhibit the development of mature, independent writing skills, raising concerns about authenticity and academic integrity. These findings underscore a critical point: current AI tools, due to their correction-based designs and lack of transparent, explanation-driven feedback, often encourage passive reliance. This powerfully reinforces the necessity for systems like SynTutor that are designed to promote active learning by helping users understand grammatical principles rather than simply providing corrections.

## **2.3 SYNTHESIS** {#2.3-synthesis}

The transition from the theoretical literature review (Chapter 2) to the technical methodology (Chapter 3) is seamless because SynTutor's architecture directly addresses each identified research gap. The "black box" problem in GEC tools, documented across Bryant et al. (2023), Li et al. (2023, 2025), Muralidaran (2022), and Cano et al. (2024), is solved by SynTutor's separation of detection from explanation: the benepar_en3 neural constituency parser fixes the phrase-structure tree before any rule fires, the Deterministic Linguistic Constraint Matrix evaluates every edge against hand-authored rules, and the Positive-Evidence Licensing layer demands positive morphological evidence for every open-class word. The pedagogical gap identified by Bochari & Hastini (2025) and Cantilero/Cartilero (2024, 2025)—that visual syntax trees are effective but not integrated into automated AI systems—is closed by SynTutor's interactive HTML5 Canvas renderer paired with deterministic XAI explanations. The transparency gap described by Farrow (2023) and Ribeiro-Flucht et al. (2024) is addressed through SynTutor's Hybrid Acceptability/Correction-Search channel, which combines a CoLA acceptability classifier with deterministic correction search, never allowing the trained model to invent a rule violation.
