## Exceptions and anti-false-positive audit

1. **Printed p. 203:** The Cambridge account accepts Dialect B *may* as a growing **Standard English variant**, not a mechanical error or a preterite inflection. Perfect *have* after a modal can have **internal** scope (past event under a current modal assessment) or **external** scope (past modal possibility). The deontic external-perfect paradigm includes *need, ought, should, might, could* but not *must*; epistemic *must have* remains grammatical and must not be condemned.
2. **Printed p. 204:** *Needn't have* and *might have* can each shift from internal to external perfect depending on whether the modality is epistemic or deontic/dynamic. Negative or positive external perfects often imply an **opposite-polarity actual fact**, but *should have* implications can be uncertain or cancellable. Some unrealised risk and wish *could/might have* examples do entail counterfactuality. Strong/medium internal-negation and weak external-negation are **tendencies**, subject to negative auxiliary *need*, epistemic/existential *may*, separated/prosodically marked *may/can*, and interrogative-specific exceptions on p. 205.
3. **Printed p. 205:** Biased **negative interrogatives** have external negation even with strong/medium auxiliaries; tags likewise question the whole positive modal claim. Positive interrogative auxiliary *need* is licensed although affirmative main-clause auxiliary *need* is otherwise restricted. Negative permission responses are normally *needn't*, not *mustn't*, when the meaning is absence of obligation. Tentative *could/might* requests may receive answers with *can/may*. *Why should* can ask for deontic justification **or** challenge a presupposed fact.
4. **Printed p. 206:** *Have (got) to* tends to report **objective** deontic requirements more than subjective *must*, and has dynamic uses. Its **epistemic** use is common in AmE and comparatively rare in BrE, not ungrammatical. Quasi-modal *be* supports strong deontic and **passive-only weak dynamic** interpretations. Past schedules with *was to* do **not** by themselves entail occurrence, unlike a special formal narrative future-in-the-past *was to*. *Was to have* may suggest nonactualisation. Conditional *are to* conveys purpose, while remote *were to* can reinforce remoteness. Lexical *don't need to* and auxiliary *needn't* can overlap despite differing preferred readings.
5. **Printed p. 207:** Negative past lexical *didn't need to* does not assert completion, while deontic *needn't have* strongly suggests completion. *Necessary* uses objective deontic rather than epistemic necessity and accepts a mandative or infinitival complement. *Necessarily* is often objective epistemic, including nonaffirmative contexts; *surely* is epistemic and outside negative scope, whereas *certainly* may convey an emphatic **direct knowledge** commitment rather than inference. *Bound to* is generally strong epistemic but can be deontic, changing negative scope. A past *was obliged to* usually entails completed action, while *had an obligation to* does not. Medium *probably/should* harmony is grammatical.
6. **Printed p. 208:** *Supposed/meant to* often report **objective** medium deontic obligations; although *not* is matrix-positioned, it normally has **internal semantic scope**, and epistemic *supposed* also occurs. Negative *want* tends toward internal meaning, not an error of word order. *Able to* is narrower than *can* (not subjective permission, existential capacity, or actualised present perception). Negative *willing* strongly suggests nonactualisation but does not entail it; compulsion can override refusal. *Allow/permit/let* can mean **causation**, not just permission; their present-tense performative use is limited, and external negation can be cancelled by identifying another permitting cause.
7. **Printed p. 209:** The **CGEL analysis rejects a future-tense category in English**; rejecting a traditional classification is not rejecting any valid sentence with *will*. *Would* is the preterite of *will*, with past time, backshift and remote uses; its existence makes the traditional one-dimensional past/present/future table inadequate. Both auxiliary syntax and modal semantics reinforce the classification of *will/shall* with *can/may/must*. Simple present and *will* may refer to the same future event with distinct epistemic strengths.
8. **Printed p. 210:** Both present-tense *will* and *may* admit clauses about past, present and future situations through perfect aspect and complement interpretation. Dynamic negative *won't* may express **present refusal** or the current properties of an object. Similar-looking present/future contrasts in other languages do not establish an English future tense. English future-time meaning also occurs in **imperatives, mandatives, present futurates, future-time subordinate present clauses, bare infinitival, to-infinitival and gerund-participial clauses**. *Be going to* begins at the bottom of p. 210, but its extended analysis is on p. 211 and is explicitly left for the next extraction.
