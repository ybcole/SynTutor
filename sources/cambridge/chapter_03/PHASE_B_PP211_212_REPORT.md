## Guarded exceptions and interpretive distinctions

1. **Infinitival and literal-motion structure (p. 211):** future *be going to* is an idiom with a **to-infinitival complement** and no literal motion implication; literal progressive *going* is separately grammatical. A tightly associated spoken reduction sometimes written *gonna* exists in some varieties and registers. English examples with ordinary *going to the museum* as physical travel must not be recategorised as automatically ill formed.
2. **Style and inflection (p. 211):** *be going to* is comparatively informal, *will* neutral; this is not a universal grammatical prohibition. *Be* can be present, past or selected as a nonfinite form after other auxiliaries; double-progressive *being going* is excluded in this future idiom. This is not a ban on literal gerund-participial *going* in other environments.
3. **Two times in the idiom (p. 211):** the **matrix** time comes from *be*, and the **infinitival event** lies later relative to it. A past *was going to* can therefore project an event that is past relative to the speech time. The difference in focus from *will* is a tendency, not a strict grammatical substitution rule.
4. **Immediate evidence and intention (pp. 211–212):** present danger and visible signs often favour *going to*; context may also support a prearranged instruction. With *will*, a hypothetical request or other condition may be more salient. *Be going to* can still involve a suppressing condition (unless an event is stopped), so classifying these examples merely by the presence of an overt *if* is unsafe.
5. **Conditionals (p. 211, footnote 67):** future-oriented *going to* is suitable in *if* antecedents describing present signs or a **goal/purpose-oriented** necessity. Pure futurity *will* does not substitute in such purpose clauses. *Will* is possible in other *if* clauses where it expresses objective predictability or volition; that distinction is preserved as a guard from prior chapter pages.
6. **Long-run plans (p. 212):** an event several years away can use *be going to* when an **intention or arrangement exists now**. Stronger present focus does not eliminate future-time adjuncts. The difference from *will* is weaker than the contrast between the present perfect and the simple preterite.
7. **Future-in-past actualisation (p. 212):** narrative *would* normally entails that the later event occurred, unlike *was going to*, which only reports a past intention or arrangement. *Was going to* frequently suggests nonactualisation but does not entail it. *Had been going to* strengthens that implication without making it a logical entailment (footnote 69). A checker must not mark *was going to* as an error merely because a later event happened.
8. **Immediate futurity (p. 212, footnote 68):** *be about to* and the less frequent *be on the point of* encode a very short interval before the event, whereas *be going to* allows a wider gap. Temporal *already* and *just* can foreground the matrix time with *going to/about to* but not in the same way with futurity *will*.
9. **Intention versus refusal (p. 212):** negated *be going to* tends toward intention not to act; *won't* more clearly indicates refusal. A past *wasn't going to* is compatible with a **subsequent change of mind**, whereas *wouldn't* tends to stress refusal. These are not categorical error checks.
