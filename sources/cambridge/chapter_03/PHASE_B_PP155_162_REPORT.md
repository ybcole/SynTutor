## Source exceptions and false-positive safeguards

- **Backshift is frequently optional (pp. 155–158).** Backshifted past content uses the earlier speaker's orientation; unshifted present uses the reporter's orientation. A persistent state may make both equally appropriate in context. A recent headache, scheduled future departure, or already-expired promise can change what the tense entails.
- **Original tense matters (p. 156).** Present, preterite and present perfect can each be reported with backshift or unshifted forms, whereas an already past-perfect statement has no further triple-past tense form. In nested reports, backshifting must occur in a continuous prefix from the top down *for the same original temporal relation*. A separate truly past subordinate fact must not be flagged by spelling alone.
- **Tense choice depends on discourse (pp. 157–158).** Reporter endorsement, rejection, contrast between old and current beliefs, present-perfect reporting clauses, and economy of expression influence whether backshift is chosen. These are preferences, not blanket grammar prohibitions. For an originally past stative report, the past perfect may be needed to distinguish original past from backshifted present.
- **The primary/secondary tense distinction is structural (p. 159).** Preterite is inflectional and more strongly grammaticalised, whereas perfect is analytic and can express anteriority when primary past is unavailable, already used for modality, or used for backshift. The contrast between perfect and non-perfect is not a separate inflectional "non-perfect tense".
- **Non-finite verbs do not fix temporal reference (pp. 159–162).** Gerund-participial complements and NP modifiers take time reference from matrix meaning, deictic context, or adjuncts. A finite-present, simultaneous dynamic reading often receives a habitual interpretation, but future-oriented may permits a one-off event. Under understand, a present schedule can describe a future event.
- **Catenatives impose distinct selection restrictions (pp. 160–161).** Remember with gerund-participial versus infinitival complement is not automatically interchangeable. A perfect-headed non-finite clause is permitted by many catenatives but excluded with tightly temporally bound begin and causative make in the cited uses. Crucially, lexical *have* and valid perfects under *seem*, *expect*, or *want* are explicit negative guards.
- **NP modifiers and progressive (p. 162).** Gerund- and passive past-participial modifiers may be past, present, or future depending on context. Infinitival modifiers are more restricted and may describe an actual event or an unfulfilled possibility. The beginning of §8 defines the core progressive as *be + gerund-participle*; details continuing on pp. 163 onward have not been extracted in this batch.
