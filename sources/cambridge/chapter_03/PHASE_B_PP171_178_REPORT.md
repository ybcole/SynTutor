## Exceptions and contrasts retained from the source

1. **Position is not a fixed aspectual class (p. 171):** a permanent spatial relation usually favours a non-progressive state; a temporary animate position can be dynamic and progressive. Narrative may use progressive for a permanent landmark through the observer's temporarily limited viewpoint. A temporary past position can use simple preterite as a bounded occurrence.
2. **Progressive futurate is a special future interpretation (pp. 171–172):** it tends to involve human intention or agency and relatively near future events. Ordinary simple-present futurates can instead describe predictable natural processes or institutionally set schedules. The progressive is broader than an externally scheduled plan when an individual has formed a private intention. Scheduled medical procedures may permit both futurate constructions without meaningful difference.
3. **Will plus progressive has two distinct readings (pp. 171–172):** genuine progress-at-a-future-reference-time and a non-aspectual already-settled future. A when-clause may therefore identify a mid-interval in one reading or the event's start in another. The non-aspectual reading often avoids a request, unwillingness or immediate-decision interpretation. Similar effects can occur after be going to.
4. **Mood differs from modality (pp. 172–174):** English's remaining singular irrealis were is a residual inflectional distinction; the larger English mood system is analytic, expressed with modal auxiliaries. Lexical adjectives, adverbs, verbs, and nouns can convey modality without being NICE-capable auxiliaries. Modal necessity can express objective mathematics or another person's perspective, rather than only the present speaker's private conviction.
5. **Actualisation and subordination (p. 174):** prospective infinitives often contrast with actual gerund-participial complements, but the selecting predicate matters; the forms are not unconditional nonfactual/factual markers. Imperative and interrogative clause types carry default modal associations, but speech acts are context-sensitive. Subordination under think does not commit the narrator to the reported truth, while know is factive. Mandative plain form and should are both licensed; remote conditional matrices are normally modalised.
6. **Internal and external negation are semantically distinct (pp. 175–176):** internal negation applies to the situation inside the modal's scope; external negation denies modal necessity or possibility itself. Both may co-occur (e.g., two negations with impossibility), and should not be falsely flagged as ungrammatical double negation. Mustn't conveys a prohibition, whereas needn't conveys non-obligation. Lexical paraphrase can reveal scope but may be less idiomatic.
7. **Logical equivalence is not always pragmatic identity (pp. 176–177):** necessary P is equivalent to impossible not-P at the appropriate scopal analysis, and not necessary P means possible not-P. An assertive must can function as a warm invitation, while a permissive may can function as an order. The strength of the modal semantics and force of the speech act must not be collapsed.
8. **Medium modality has its own gradation (p. 177):** epistemic should and ought express probability stronger than the possibility expressed by may but weaker than epistemic must. The pragmatic reading of not likely and likely not usually converges, even though their literal scope differs. Probably not is the central ordering in an ordinary simple clause; marginal alternatives are not automatic errors.
9. **Modality kind and strength are independent (p. 178):** epistemic modality concerns inferred knowledge, deontic modality concerns permission/obligation and a deontic source, and dynamic modality concerns participant abilities and dispositions. Must, may and can can have distinct kinds or genuine ambiguity. The time associations (epistemic present/past, deontic future) are prototypical, not absolute.
