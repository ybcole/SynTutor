## Explicit exception and risk audit

1. **187, should/ought:** Epistemic should often includes normative expectation but fully deontic readings need not predict events. Unfavourable outcomes and inferences from known effects to hidden causes disfavor the epistemic reading but do not create blanket grammatical bans. Negative should/ought normally takes internal scope; the low-degree use belongs to should rather than an unconstrained general ought.
2. **188, special should:** Mandative should pairs with deontic predicates spanning strong and medium force. Emotive should usually allows a non-modal equivalent but not always, especially with potential future events; rhetorical question should has a special emotive role. Conditional should adds some doubt and is unnatural for an incontrovertible premise; open and remote matrix clauses both occur. Central-epistemic will normally has second- or third-person subjects, not a rule banning first-person futurity.
3. **189, inference:** Central-epistemic will is strong semantically yet typically weaker pragmatically than direct assertion. It may express subjective assumption or objective logical consequence. Must more clearly expresses a conclusion or explanation, will more readily suggests later confirmation. Will in the present can be confused with future-time prediction. Neither semantic certainty nor pragmatic confidence alone is an error detector.
4. **190, futurity:** A birthday or rapidly approaching nightfall can make will near a time marker, without removing all contingency. Competitive outcomes instead produce a genuine prediction. A simple present like an already-settled fixture needs stronger evidence than will for an unconfirmed matchup. The existence of a valid present futurate is **not** required for will to have low modal degree.
5. **191, temporal constraint:** The book explicitly marks pure futurity will invalid in future-time temporal adjuncts with when/as soon as and similar expressions; present-tense verbs are required. This yielded **one** diagnostic specification, OTH-377, still **spec-only**. Crucial guards include embedded interrogatives (e.g., *Tell me when the courier will arrive*), rare epistemic will in conditional *if* protases (where present predictability is the condition), and volitional will in *if* clauses. A detector must distinguish temporal adjuncts from embedded interrogatives and modal uses before activation.
6. **191–192, consequences:** Conditional consequences may refer to past, present, or future; will is optional and often pragmatically weaker than corresponding unmodalised tenses. The simple present in a future consequence can heighten a categorical warning. Adverbs with will carry independent modal meaning, not harmony of the kind found with must or may; negative won't normally has internal negation.
7. **193, dynamic will:** Will is stronger than want/intend, yet volition is frequently an implicature of agent-controlled futurity. The pragmatic commitment of a first-person promise must not be confused with grammatical obligation. Present refusal (won't) and strongly stressed determination increase modal salience; closed second-person will questions can pragmatically issue requests. Volitional will in an if-clause is permitted.
8. **194, propensity and shall:** Failure to start or fit permits metaphorical unwillingness for inanimates. Will may express recurring behaviour or generic physical properties, often close to simple present, and stress conveys affect. Authoritative will/be going to can imply commands because of the relationship between speaker and addressee. Regulative shall is restricted to legal or similar settings, while guarantor shall imposes responsibility on the speaker. Further shall uses continue beyond p. 194.
