## Source-supported distinctions and conservative guards

- A simple preterite may express anteriority relative to a future matrix orientation; it need not be interpreted as prior to speech. Past progressive futurates refer to plans held at a former time.
- Perfect is a secondary anteriority system. Preterite perfect contains an intermediate past orientation; present perfect has a primary present reference, whereas the simple preterite refers to past time directly.
- The diagram on p. 141 confirms three distinct time structures. Continuative perfect can span up to orientation time; non-continuative perfect locates an occurrence earlier in the span. Overt since/for is common but not mandatory in every context.
- Non-progressive continuative perfect normally concerns atelic situations; an ordinary since phrase may also permit a non-continuative reading, and progressive aspect can change the interpretation of a telic event.
- Present perfect normally excludes adverbials locating one completed event at a definite past time wholly before now. The source explicitly discusses restricted experiential uses, including already plus a past expression, as exceptions. Just/recently are not equivalent to ago.
- Experiential, resultative and recent-past perfect readings overlap. The continued existence of a result is generally an implicature, not an entailment, and may be cancelled by subsequent events.
- American English and British English can prefer different tense choices with just, already and yet. Do not treat these dialectal preferences as unconditional ungrammaticality.
