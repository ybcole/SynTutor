## Verified qualifications and false-positive protections

1. **179:** Dynamic/deontic categories have a fuzzy boundary; the absence of a deontic source, not the spelling of can, motivates a dynamic reading. Modal *degree* and *strength* are independent. Concessive may, exhaustive-conditional may and emotive should have different licensing contexts.
2. **180:** Modal-adverb pairs may be harmonic when strengths match; an unlike pair can instead have nested scope and is not ungrammatical. Low-degree futurate will is not necessarily harmony. Nonaffirmative auxiliary need is distinct from lexical need to; epistemic can is restricted without restricting dynamic can.
3. **181:** Negative epistemic mustn't/needn't are rare or rejected by some speakers, not universal errors. Subjective must is pragmatically weaker than objective logical necessity, but affirmative epistemic necessity still semantically entails its complement. Can't is more categorical than an accepted negative inferential mustn't. May well can strengthen may.
4. **182:** May's implication of incomplete knowledge is cancellable, particularly in concessive clauses. Future epistemic may is ordinary, whereas future epistemic can is strongly restricted and future must typically deontic; exceptions are documented. Source distinguishes necessity, possibility, deontic permissions and social force.
5. **183:** Mustn't prohibits, needn't removes obligation; deontic may not/can't usually prohibit. Internal-negation permissive readings are marginal and may require prosodic separation. Subjective permission presupposes authority; may/can style alone does not identify subjective versus objective source. The fixed phrase *if I may* is not freely replaceable by *if I can*.
6. **184:** Deontic obligations ordinarily target future actualisation, but a standing rule can require past prerequisites. First-person must can impose a self-chosen duty. Dynamic can includes reasonable expectations, physical possibility and existential variability. The peripheral deontic uses **may as well** (fallback suggestion) and **optative may** (wish construction) were checked but not inflated into productive correction rules.
7. **185:** Existential can can convey occasional truth; with or it may suggest, but does not entail, an exhaustive list. Formal existential may admits a possible-negated reading. Ability can does not license may as the same-meaning substitute, but may can still express permission/epistemic possibility. Potential ability must not be equated with repeated actualisation. Dynamic must is relatively rare; *must needs* is an idiom.
8. **186:** Idiomatic should is not generally analysable as shall + ordinary preterite. Should/ought are commonly interchangeable for medium-strength deontic and epistemic readings, but only should is normal for indirect instructions. Epistemic should expresses an inference; past-time perfect complements mostly invite deontic evaluation, though the restriction is described as very strong rather than absolute.
